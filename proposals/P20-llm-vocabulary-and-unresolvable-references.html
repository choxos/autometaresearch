Do papers that read like a language model cite references that do not exist?

design
aitext
integrity
Whether excess language-model vocabulary in a published article predicts references that resolve to nothing, after 2023 and not before.
Published

September 22, 2026

ImportantStatus: design. No probe has run.

Written against the design standard. The author maintains Citicious, a browser extension that flags retracted and possibly hallucinated citations; this design tests a relation in the published literature rather than building a detector, and may reuse its matching logic, which would be disclosed.

1. The question

Language models invent references, and that is documented for chatbot output. Whether invented references have reached the published literature, and whether they concentrate in articles whose prose shows the vocabulary that language models overuse, is a different question. It asks about what has been published, not what a chatbot produces when asked.

2. Prior work, and the search behind the novelty claim

One hundred and seventy-six PubMed records. The relevant ones measure fabricated references in chatbot output: for systematic review queries (PMID 38776130) and for medical content (PMID 37337480). The relevance-ordered top ten contains no study relating vocabulary in published articles to reference validity.

3. Hypothesis

In articles published from 2023, a higher vocabulary-excess score is associated with a larger share of references that cannot be resolved; in articles published before 2023 it is not.

Null. The interaction between vocabulary excess and period is 0.

What would falsify it. No interaction: an association of the same size before 2023 would mean the score measures something other than model use, such as field or writing style, and the pre-2023 period is included precisely as that negative control.

4. Design and unit

Cross-sectional with a pre-period negative control. One article is one unit.

5. Sample

PMC open-access research articles from 2019 to 2025, sampled evenly by year. A development slice of 10 percent, drawn first and never analyzed, is used only to build the vocabulary score (S1).

6. Exposure

Vocabulary-excess score. On the development slice, the words whose frequency per million tokens in 2024 exceeds their 2021 frequency by a ratio fixed in advance form the marker list. The score for an article is the marker frequency in its body. Built once, frozen, and published with the protocol before the analysis slice is touched.

7. Outcome and instrument

The proportion of an article’s references that cannot be resolved. A reference with a DOI in pub-id[@pub-id-type='doi'] is resolved against Crossref. A reference without one is matched on title against Crossref and OpenAlex with a similarity threshold. Unresolved references are further split into malformed DOIs, well-formed DOIs that resolve to nothing, and titles with no match.

The denominator is references the method can attempt. Book chapters, reports and web pages are counted and excluded, since failing to match a report is not evidence it does not exist.

8. Validation gate

The author checks 200 unresolved references by hand against library catalogues and search engines, coding each as real, garbled real, or nonexistent. The primary outcome is recomputed as the proportion coded nonexistent; if fewer than 20 percent of unresolved references are nonexistent, unresolvability measures metadata quality rather than invention and the hypothesis is tested on the hand-checked subset only.

9. Analysis

In R.

glmmTMB(cbind(unresolved, attempted - unresolved) ~ score * post2023 + (1 | journal),
        family = betabinomial, data = d)

Preregistered alongside: the same model using the hand-coded nonexistent share on the validation subset, and a model excluding journals with fewer than 20 sampled articles.

12. What this cannot show

  • Whether any individual article used a language model.
  • Invented references that happen to resolve to a real but irrelevant paper.
