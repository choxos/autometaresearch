Do meta-research studies follow the guidelines they audit others by?

design
report
reflexive
The reflexive reporting question. Frame counted; the scoring instrument needs a human.
Published

September 16, 2026

ImportantStatus: design. Frame counted. The scoring instrument is the obstacle. Not a protocol.

The frame is large: 630 tier A records and 685 frame records declare a cross-sectional or methodological design, of which 67 and 197 respectively name a reporting guideline.

What blocks this from being a protocol is not data but measurement: scoring adherence to STROBE means reading 22 items per article, and no validated automatic scorer exists. Section 5 is honest that this is the most expensive study of the set per unit of evidence.

1. The question

Meta-research grades other people’s papers against reporting checklists. Most meta-research is itself a cross-sectional study of a sample of articles, and cross-sectional studies have a checklist: STROBE. A field that measures adherence and does not meet it is making an argument against its own instrument.

2. Hypothesis

Meta-research articles that themselves assess guideline adherence report their own methods more completely than meta-research articles that do not.

The hypothesis is deliberately internal rather than a comparison against non-meta-research, because a comparison across fields would confound topic with venue with design. This is the same structure as P01v2, and the same criticism applies to it.

Primary null. Adherence does not differ by whether the article’s own subject is reporting quality.

What would falsify it. No difference, or worse adherence among the auditors.

3. Sample

Articles from the tier A corpus and the tier B frame whose design is cross-sectional or methodological, with PMC full text.

frame declaring such a design naming a reporting guideline
tier A 630 67
tier B frame 685 197

Design is determined from the full text methods section, not from the abstract regex used for this count. The regex is a frame estimate and is labeled as one.

4. Exposure

Whether the article’s own subject is reporting quality, from the report topic label.

5. Outcome, and why this is expensive

A STROBE-based adherence score over the items that apply to a study of documents rather than of people. Not all 22 items apply; the applicable subset is fixed in advance and published with the protocol, because choosing which items apply after scoring is how an adherence study manufactures its own result.

Scoring requires reading. The realistic options are:

  1. The author scores a random sample of 150 articles. Precise, and the ceiling on sample size.
  2. Models score all of them after being validated against the author’s 150, gated at kappa 0.70 per item group, with items that fail the gate dropped and reported as dropped.

Option 2 is the design, with option 1 as its reference standard, and neither can start until the author’s 150 exist.

6. Analysis

In R. Linear regression of the adherence proportion on the exposure, adjusted for year and with journal as a random intercept; and a per-item logistic model so that a difference driven by one item is visible rather than averaged away.

Deviations

Recorded on the result page: what changed, when, why, and the estimate before and after where that can be computed. A deviation is not a failure; concealing one is.

What this cannot show

  • Reporting completeness is not methodological quality and the study never claims it is.
  • A well-reported study can be wrong, and a badly reported one right.
