Meta-research on transparency shares its own data more often

result
datashare
reflexive
Result for P01. Papers whose subject is data sharing are about four times more likely to point at a repository of their own.
Published

September 16, 2026

ImportantThis result is exploratory, not confirmatory

It was run against a one-page proposal rather than a full protocol, and it has not been reviewed by anyone. Three things follow and none of them are repairable after the fact:

  • The grading rule was developed on the analysis data. Statements were read to design it. Reading was blind to the exposure, which limits the damage, but a rule that has seen the data it will be applied to is not a pre-specified instrument.
  • Three of four sensitivity analyses are post hoc, two of them invented in response to defects found during the run.
  • Hand validation has not been done, so the rule’s agreement with a person is unknown.

The one property that does hold is the important one: the protocol was published and timestamped in git before the analysis ran, so the question could not be redefined after the answer was known. That makes this a real finding worth following up. It does not make it a confirmatory test, and it should not be cited as one.

A full protocol is at P01-v2, written after this run, which is why any result under it has to come from data this analysis did not use.

Analysis run by Claude Opus 5 on 2026-09-16. Code at studies/P01-advocacy-and-practice/.

Result

Among 999 meta-research articles with full text in PMC, those whose own subject is data sharing, code availability or FAIR data point at a repository of their own about four times as often as meta-research articles on any other subject.

none declarative actionable
other topic (n = 956) 43.0% 36.5% 20.5%
transparency topic (n = 43) 32.6% 16.3% 51.2%

Adjusted for publication year and article type, with journal as a random intercept:

Odds ratio 4.40 (95% CI 1.80 to 10.75), p = 0.0012.

The preregistered ordinal model on all three levels agrees: 0.99 on the log-odds scale (p = 0.011).

The hypothesis stated in the protocol was directional and it is supported. Advocacy and practice are not independent inside this field.

What the grades mean

grade meaning
none no data availability statement in the article’s front or back matter
declarative a statement that shares nothing: on request, within the article, not applicable
actionable a named repository, accession, DOI or URL a reader can follow without asking

Two defects found during the run, and what each cost

Both were found by checking the result rather than by the code failing. Neither raised an error, and the first produced a plausible answer that was partly an artifact.

The extractor read papers’ own results as their statements

A meta-research paper about data availability contains sections called “Data availability over time” and “Extraction of data availability status” in its own methods and results. The first extractor walked the whole article, found those, and graded the paper actionable because such sections are full of repository URLs. Four of the 43 exposed records were contaminated, and the signature was visible from outside: the exposed arm hit the fuzzy title-matching routes three times more often than the unexposed arm.

A publisher-assigned sec-type="data-availability" does not protect against this. One article carries that attribute on a numbered methods section. Location does protect against it: a data availability statement is front or back matter, and a numbered section of the narrative is the paper’s argument rather than its disclosure. Extraction is now restricted to <front> and <back>.

The repair was not purely subtractive. One article’s real statement sits in <front> and was being shadowed by its own results section.

Cost: the uncorrected odds ratio was 3.86. After the fix it is higher, not lower. The artifact was not what was producing the effect, which is not what was expected when the check began.

The grading rule downgraded deposits that also offered data on request

The rule tested for an on-request clause before testing for a repository, on the reasoning that “available from the corresponding author at an email address” contains something that looks like a locator and is a refusal. That is right for a bare URL and wrong for a named repository. One article deposits its code on GitHub and OSF and separately says the raw Scopus data cannot be released under license; it shared what it could, and the rule called it declarative.

A named repository now outranks a co-occurring on-request clause. Six of 574 statements carry both, five of them a named repository.

Cost: all six are in the unexposed arm, so the correction raises the comparison rate and works against the hypothesis. The odds ratio fell from 4.67 to 4.40.

Sensitivity

Three of these four analyses are post hoc. An earlier version of this page said each was “specified for a reason stated before it was run, not selected after seeing it.” That was false and it is corrected here rather than edited away. Only the ordinal model was preregistered in P01. Two of the three sensitivities below were invented in direct response to defects found during the run, which is a reasonable thing to do and is not the same thing as having planned them.

analysis preregistered odds ratio 95% CI
primary yes 4.40 1.80 to 10.75
ordinal model, all three levels yes see above p = 0.011
publisher-typed routes only, no title matching by us no, post hoc 3.03 1.12 to 8.23
supplementary material counted as actionable no, post hoc 2.58 1.14 to 5.85
excluding the author’s own papers no, post hoc 4.10 1.65 to 10.20

The publisher-typed row is the one that matters most. The repaired bug lived in the title-matching routes, so this refits using only statements a publisher explicitly tagged, where no pattern of ours decides what counts as a statement. The effect is smaller and it survives.

The supplementary-material row relaxes the protocol’s choice to grade “included in this published article and its supplementary information files” as declarative. That choice is defensible rather than obviously right, since supplementary material is retrievable by anyone with journal access. Relaxing it moves 95 articles into the actionable arm and roughly halves the effect, which it does not remove.

The author’s own papers are in this corpus

A tier A search for meta-research retrieves the work of anyone who does meta-research, including whoever is running the study. Two articles in the analysis set name the author’s own repositories. Both are in the exposed arm and both are graded actionable, so their presence pushes the estimate in the direction of the hypothesis. Removing them changes the odds ratio from 4.40 to 4.10. Reported rather than silently dropped.

What this does not show

  • It is not causal and the design cannot make it so. A researcher who studies data sharing and a researcher who shares data may simply be the same kind of person, or may work in the same few journals with the same few policies. The journal random intercept absorbs the average journal but not an interaction between a journal’s policy and a paper’s topic.
  • 43 exposed records is thin. The smallest effect this design could have detected at 80 percent power was a rise from 20.5 to 48.9 percent. The observed effect is larger than that floor, which is why it is visible; an effect half this size would have been missed, and a null here would have ruled out very little.
  • The exposure is a vocabulary match, not a judgment. A paper labelled as a transparency topic may mention data sharing once in passing. That misclassification is non-differential with respect to the outcome and biases toward the null, so it weakens this finding rather than manufacturing it.
  • 60 percent PMC coverage is not random. 742 corpus records have no PMCID and 89 more are in PMC as metadata only. Open-access availability is itself correlated with transparency practice, so the sample leans transparent. This is stated in the protocol and is not fixable within the design.
  • The grading rule has been validated by a model and not yet by a person. See below.

Validation still outstanding

The protocol calls for 100 statements graded by hand to measure where the rule disagrees. studies/P01-advocacy-and-practice/out/validation-sample.csv holds that sample with an empty hand_grade column, and the agreement will be reported here when it is filled in.

What has happened instead is weaker and is labeled as such: 40 statements were read and graded by the same model that wrote the rule, which found the second defect above. A model checking its own rule is not an independent check, and the kappa against a person is not yet known.

Reproducing

python3 studies/P01-advocacy-and-practice/extract.py   # PMC full text -> statements.csv
Rscript studies/P01-advocacy-and-practice/R/analyze.R  # grading, models, sensitivity

The extractor caches PMC responses by batch content. data/statements.csv is tracked, so the analysis reruns without touching NCBI.
